On [July 8, 2008](https://www.reuters.com/article/business/energy/transocean-rig-gets-record-650000-dayrate-idUSN08177559/), **Transocean (`NYSE: RIG`)** [disclosed in an SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1083269/000119312508148448/dex991.htm) a deal that stunned Wall Street: oil giant Eni had agreed to rent a single ultra-deepwater drillship, the *Deepwater Pathfinder*, for a record **\$650,000 a day** over five years.

At the time, oil was above \$140 a barrel and the world was desperate for rigs. Transocean’s contract backlog topped \$40 billion, its stock traded near **\$160**, and the math looked bulletproof: borrow hundreds of millions of dollars to build a ship, lock in a supermajor on a five-year take-or-pay contract, and print money.

When that five-year contract expired in March 2015, newer-generation drillships had flooded the market. Nobody wanted an older rig. Transocean [parked the *Pathfinder* twelve miles off the coast of Trinidad](https://gcaptain.com/oils-biggest-rigs-get-sent-to-junkyard-as-daily-losses-mount/) with its engines off for two years before [sending it to the scrapyard in September 2017](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1451505/000145150517000063/c505-20170922x8k.htm). Transocean is still public today—nearly twenty years later, **`RIG` trades at \$5.62, down 96% from its peak.** The five-year contracts were real, the customers paid on time, and the debtholders were made whole—but shareholders got wiped out.

![Share prices: Transocean (RIG) peaked in 2008 and fell 96%; AerCap (AER), an aircraft lessor, kept climbing](/images/posts/coreweave/rig_vs_aer.png)

Now you can see the remarkable resemblance between Transocean and neo-cloud companies like **CoreWeave**, whose business model can be summarized as:

> You pay $X$ amount of money for a computer chip with borrowed money, then rent the chip out by the hour and hope it earns back more than the loan costs before it becomes worthless.

When your business is using 90% debt to rent out assets, it's going to end in one of two ways. The good way is if the asset has a long useful life and doesn't go obsolete--like a Boeing 737, which flies for 25 years and pays off its loan 3 times over. The bad way is if the assets are rendered obsolete before the debt is even paid off. 

In the next few sections, we'll try to find out which case CoreWeave belongs to. If the details are too boring, feel free to jump to the end where I assembled an interactive model for you to test your own hypothesis. After all, future is not predictable and we are all here watching history being made live.

## Model

To understand why CoreWeave's stocks can potentially be worth \$0, we need to figure out a couple important variables:

* **Upfront Cost ($X$):** The all-in capital expenditure to buy the chip, networking gear, and plug it into a data center.

* **Hourly Rent ($P$), Contract Length ($T$), and Capacity Utilization ($u$):** How much a customer pay per hour and how long a contract lasts, and to what extent the capacity is being utilized.

* **Cash Operating Cost ($c$):** What it costs per hour just to keep the lights on—electricity, data center rent, and maintenance.

* **The "Aging Curve" — Price Decay ($\delta$) & Lifespan ($L$):** Once the first 5-year contract ends, how fast the chip's rental price drops each year ($\delta$) as newer chips arrive, and the maximum economic life ($L$, ~8 years) before it becomes e-waste.

* **Cost of Debt ($r$):** The interest rate on the borrowed money (~9–10% on marginal secured debt). 

For a single chip (or megawatt of chips) to create any value at all, the present value of its net rental cash flows over its lifespan must beat its upfront cost:

$$

\text{NPV} = \underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{P \cdot u - c}{(1+r)^t}}_{\text{Initial Contract}} + \underbrace{\sum_{t=T+1}^{L} \frac{P(1-\delta)^{t-1} \cdot u - c}{(1+r)^t}}_{\text{Post-Contract Tail (Residual Value)}} - X

$$

And to get what your **stock** is worth, you add up the NPV of all those chips and subtract the massive mountain of debt and customer prepayments (right now it's around $D_{\text{net}} \approx$ \$38.6B) sitting ahead of equity:

$$

\text{Equity Value} = \sum \text{NPV}_{\text{chips}} - D_{\text{net}}

$$

## Data

Every number in the model can be traced to something public: CoreWeave's own filings, contract announcements from it and its competitors, and GPU rental price indices. I won't walk through all of them here (the full list lives in the Data tab), but two examples show how the pieces get pinned down.

**Example 1: what a chip rents for ($P$), and what it costs ($X$).**

CoreWeave doesn't publish its contract prices. But in November 2025 one of its competitors, IREN, announced a five-year, \$9.7 billion deal with Microsoft for roughly 76,000 of Nvidia's GB300 chips. Spread that evenly over every hour of the contract:

$$
P \approx \frac{\$9.7\text{B}}{5\ \text{yrs} \times 76{,}000\ \text{GPUs} \times 8{,}760\ \text{hrs/yr}} \approx \$2.91\ \text{per GPU-hour}
$$

That's what a hyperscaler pays when it signs for five years and tens of thousands of chips at once, and it's our anchor for $P$ on CoreWeave's big contracts. The same announcement also gives us $X$: IREN ordered \$5.8 billion of servers and networking gear from Dell for those chips, about \$76,000 per GPU. The model actually uses a lower figure, about \$69,000 all-in, which is generous to CoreWeave.

**Example 2: how fast old chips lose value ($\delta$ and $L$).**

The best evidence is the A100, the chip behind the first ChatGPT, launched in 2020. When it was new, CoreWeave's own public price list charged \$2.21 an hour for it. Today, Silicon Data's A100 rental index sits at about \$1.53. Over almost six years, that works out to

$$
\delta \approx 1 - \left(\frac{1.53}{2.21}\right)^{1/5.8} \approx 6\%\ \text{per year}
$$

The H100, two years younger, shows about 5% a year. The model uses 7%, since the old list price left out the CPU and memory that are now bundled into the index.

Lifespan $L$ comes from the same data, with a twist. Silicon Data also estimates what a used A100 is worth, and that estimate fell 46% in 18 months while the A100's rent barely moved. The chip didn't get cheaper to rent; it just had fewer years left to earn. Silicon Data treats every chip as done eight years after launch (for the A100, May 2028), so that's what we use for $L$.

**Everything else**, briefly:

| Variable             | Where it comes from                                                                                                                       |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| $T$, contract length | CoreWeave's annual report: contracts average about five years                                                                             |
| $u$, utilization     | Assumed 95%; take-or-pay contracts bill close to 100%                                                                                     |
| $c$, operating cost  | CoreWeave's actual cash costs per megawatt over the past year, excluding stock compensation (about \$3.8M per MW per year)                |
| $r$, cost of debt    | CoreWeave's latest chip-backed loan, priced at SOFR + 5.5% (about 9.4%), rounded up to 10%                                                |
| $D_{\text{net}}$     | CoreWeave's June 2026 balance sheet plus debt raised since: \$28.7B of net debt, \$9.7B of customer prepayments, \$0.2B of finance leases |
